How a stage earns it

Every stage climbs the same ladder, on evidence

I wire in what I have one stage at a time. A stage gets more autonomy only when the run log shows it has earned it, and Merge climbs last.

StageLevel nowEarns the next level when
Intakelevel 0 · manualIt marks issues ready or not, and its lane; I agree on 9 of the last 10.
Planlevel 0 · manual9 of the last 10 plans approved without edits.
Buildlevel 0 · manual9 of the last 10 PRs merged with no rework.
Checklevel 0 · manualCatches every planted defect, and fails good work at most 1 in 10. Plus every planted defect caught.
Reviewlevel 0 · manualMy reviewer flags what I flagged on 9 of the last 10 PRs, and catches every planted defect. Plus every planted defect caught.
Mergelevel 0 · manualOnly once every other stage is at level 3. Then 10 merges with no revert.
The rules
  • A level counts only with evidence in the run log. No log, no level.
  • Climb on the last 10 real jobs, never on a good day.
  • Check and Review also need planted failures: known-bad changes they must catch, every one.
  • One serious miss drops the stage a level. Trust is lost faster than it is earned.
  • Merge climbs last, once every other stage is at level 3.
  • The thresholds are a starting point. The Friday review tunes them.

Draft designs

How two of the leanings would work

Drawn from where I am leaning on the cards above. They change if the decision does.

The review stack: who reads a PR before me

  1. Checks first. Nothing is reviewed until the scenario tests, lint and types pass, and the run has written what it checked.
  2. Claude’s review covers general coding practice.
  3. My reviewer knows this product: its CLAUDE.md, past decisions and history. It is strict on purpose, like the engineer who does not want the code to change.
  4. I read last, with both reviews in front of me. When I find something my reviewer missed, it becomes a new rule for it.

Risk lanes: where an issue goes

  1. Small and clear (a test, docs, a contained bug): straight to build.
  2. Ambiguous: the agent plans, and I approve the plan before any code is written.
  3. High risk (data, money, security, wide changes): stays out of the factory for now. I do it myself.

Things to watch

Where factories go wrong, and what I will do about it

  • Too many PRs to review · ReviewSmall issues, green checks first, a cap on open factory PRs.
  • Agents edit tests to pass · CheckScenario tests as the gate; lock the test folder; flag every test change in the PR.
  • The codebase slowly gets worse · MergeRead the code, not only the diff, in the Friday review. One factory was shut down after 3 months for this.
  • Instructions hidden in issues · IntakeOnly issues I wrote or labelled ready are taken; no secrets in the run.
  • Tasks too big for one run · PlanSplit the issue. Raising the turn limit did not work for others.
  • Green runs that are wrong · CheckFresh worktree per run; prove each gate can fail with a planted defect.
  • Slow checks cancel fast PRs · CheckRun only the tests a change affects; keep the full run for before merge.
  • Cost creep · SafetySubscription first; a spend cap before any unattended run.
  • The factory costs more than it saves · MeThe Friday number. Two bad weeks in a row and I simplify.

For my factory

What this changes for me

  • Build a reviewer per project that knows the product’s intent, and run it after Claude Code’s own review. independent-reviewer is generic; this one would not be.
  • A run does not open a PR until its own checks pass: the 95% rule, applied to agents.
  • Three open decisions get an answer here: who merges (decided by evidence over time), keeping tests honest (scenario tests), and how small a task must be (risk lanes).

Review

  1. Run your own review agent on top of the generic one

    Generic bots (CodeRabbit, Greptile, Claude) check coding best practice. They do not know your product’s intent or behaviour. Theirs waits for CodeRabbit to finish, then adds its own view.

    “Having your own review agent on top of that is better because you can design your review agent as for example for us a [Mastra] maintainer. This agent knows about [Mastra] like that’s its whole job.”
    at 35:45 placed: card: who reviews before me

  2. Make it strict, and keep improving it

    Theirs is modelled on the senior engineer who does not want the code to change. It runs the tests and lints in a sandbox. By the speaker’s count only 40 of the PRs it reviewed were approved.

    “I wanted the review agent to be like that guy at the company who’s been there for 10 years who does not want the code to change.”
    at 53:07 placed: card: who reviews before me

  3. Get a PR to 95% confidence before opening it

    Throwing half-done work at reviewers deepens the bottleneck. He calls this the single most useful thing to do today.

    “We have this rule where you need to get your PR 95% confident before even opening it.”
    at 21:12 placed: card: when may a run open a PR

Trust and throughput

  1. A factory is earned in steps, not switched on

    Automate one loop, gather evidence (green tests, no incidents, clean releases), then remove a human gate. Repeat.

    “A software factory is a spectrum of ideas it’s not just you turn something on and you have it.”
    at 4:35 placed: the ladder, and card: what counts as evidence

  2. Review is the bottleneck

    Agents make PRs fast, but throughput counts end to end. A full review queue means more made and less shipped. The idea comes from The Goal, a book on factory bottlenecks.

    at 10:00 placed: card: how will I know it is worth it

  3. Long-term quality is the real test

    No benchmark measures whether agent code still stands three months later. Blindly accepting it is the maintainer’s trap.

    “You don’t want to get slop cannon.”
    at 18:24 placed: trap: the codebase slowly gets worse

Testing

  1. Prefer scenario tests to unit tests

    Models fake unit tests. Scenario tests run end to end through how the system should and should not behave. Agents can write those too.

    “I think unit tests are a lie in this flow… because you could fake a unit test. Models love doing that.”
    at 23:30 placed: card: how do I keep the tests honest

  2. Mock the AI calls in tests

    They spent about $100 a day calling OpenAI from tests alone. AI mock (from CopilotKit) serves fixture data over HTTP instead.

    at 30:29 placed: card: how do I keep the tests honest

  3. Slow CI is a real cost

    A 16-minute CI wipes out a 5-second PR. They run only the tests a change affects and use faster runners.

    at 33:06 placed: trap: slow checks cancel fast PRs

Planning and routing

  1. Route work by risk

    Tests and docs: quick review. Ambiguous: lots of planning. High risk: a design review. Systemic: an experiment in a sandbox or staging.

    at 24:11 placed: card: how is work routed by risk

  2. Plan until you are sure enough to spend tokens

    “You plan until you get enough conviction that you want to spend tokens on it.”
    at 28:57 placed: card: how is work routed by risk

  3. Adversarial review yes, model councils no

    An agent that second-guesses everything is useful. Four models debating a plan mostly burns tokens.

    “Imagine an agent that just hates you and everything you say tries to like second guess you which I actually like.”
    at 38:41 placed: card: who reviews before me

Context and running it

  1. The issue text is not enough context

    Agents also need the git history of the area, a knowledge base, memory across sessions, and access to CI results and telemetry.

    “Why would you give them less tools than you have yourself?”
    at 25:39 placed: card: what context does each run get

  2. Agents report their own knowledge gaps

    When their agent does not know something, it opens an issue so a human can fill the gap. The knowledge base improves from use.

    at 59:34 placed: card: what context does each run get

  3. Measure the factory itself

    Queue health, what is coming in, agents running, cycle times, and cost per task.

    at 56:23 placed: card: how will I know it is worth it
